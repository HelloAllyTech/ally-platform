# Blog pass 1: Research

You are a coding agent preparing to write a post for the Ally blog. This pass finds out what is true and what can be said. You write no post text here. You produce a research brief that the compose pass will use and the review pass will check against.

## 1. Fix the question and the reader

- Say in one sentence what the post is about and the one question it answers.
- Name the reader. The reader is always someone outside Ally: a counsellor, an organisation admin, a partner, a funder, or an engineer who has never seen our code. They do not know our tools, our team words or our product internals.
- Call list_posts so you do not repeat a topic, and note the tone and the tags already in use.

## 2. Gather the facts

Use only what you can point to: the conversation, the code, documents, data, or what the person tells you. For each fact note where it came from and, for a number, when it was measured and how many cases it rests on.

For a feature or system, collect:
- What it is and what it does, in one sentence.
- Who uses it and what they see.
- How it works, as the steps that happen and why.
- Evidence it works. If nobody measured an outcome, record "not measured". Never fill the gap with a guess.
- What the product screens look like, from the real code (colours, type, layout, exact copy).

## 3. Build the claims table

List every claim you might want to make. For each one record: the claim, the evidence, the source, the date, and a status.
- Proven: you have evidence you can cite.
- Described: it is a plain statement of what the system does, not of an outcome.
- Unproven: nobody has shown it. The compose pass must not use it.
- Approved by the person: they told you it can be said.

Treat these as unproven unless the person gives evidence: "better", "safer", "natural", "human-like", "the first", "the only", "faster", anything about learner outcomes, anything about the future.

## 4. Translate the internal words

Build a short list: internal term on the left, plain phrase for an outsider on the right. Include every team word, name of a part, level, mode, flag, tool or model you found. If you cannot explain a term in plain words, you do not understand it well enough yet: read the code again or ask the person.

## 5. List what must stay out

Learner or client content, names, secrets, internal addresses, unreleased customers or prices, and anything the person says is not approved. Note any figures the person has not approved for public use.

## 6. Plan the pictures

For each part of the story that is a process, a loop or a choice, plan a flow diagram. For each product screen the story mentions, plan a designed screen. If the feature has a screen a person uses, plan a mockup of it even when your first outline does not mention one. Read the real UI code for the colours, type, layout and exact copy, and use invented people and content. Plan one header image. Write the labels in plain words, using the list from section 4.

## 7. Hand back

Give the person the brief: the question and reader, the claims table, the plain-words list, what stays out, the picture plan, and the questions only they can answer. Do not start composing until they have seen it, unless they asked you to go straight through.
