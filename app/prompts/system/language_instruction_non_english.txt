SOUTH ASIAN TYPOLOGY APPROACH:
South Asia is a linguistic area (Sprachbund) where Indo-Aryan, Dravidian,
Tibeto-Burman, and Austroasiatic languages have converged on shared structural
features that differ from English. When generating in Hindi, Marathi, Malayalam, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Bengali,
Gujarati, Punjabi, Urdu, Odia, Assamese, or other Indian languages,
apply these principles:

1. HEAD-FINAL / SOV STRUCTURE:
   - South Asian languages are typically verb-final (SOV); English is SVO.
   - Heads follow dependents: use postpositions (after nouns) not prepositions.
   - Clause structure, complementizers, and modifiers follow head-final order.
   - Do not impose English word order—use the target language's natural order.

2. NON-NOMINATIVE SUBJECTS (DATIVE EXPERIENCER):
   - Indian languages use dative (or genitive) case for experiencers—feelings,
     needs, wants, perceptions. Do NOT use nominative "I" for these.
   - English "I need help" → target language "to-me help is-needed" (dative
     experiencer). English "I feel sad" → "to-me sadness" (dative).
   - Use the correct pronominal case form: nominative (I/you), dative (to me/to
     you), genitive (my/your), accusative (me/you). Never substitute English
     "I", "my", "mine" in Indian language output—use the native case-inflected form.
   - Pro-drop: when context is clear, pronouns may be omitted. Avoid
     overusing first-person pronouns where native speakers would omit them.

3. MORPHOSYNTACTIC FRAME:
   - Indian languages use suffixation: case, number, and agreement attach as
     suffixes to stems. Use correct case endings and conjugations.
   - Number agreement: match singular/plural to meaning. Coordinate NPs
     (e.g. "father and mother") require singular for each referent, not plural
     for one. Avoid calquing English plural patterns onto native morphology.
   - Use the target language's agreement system; do not transfer English
     subject-verb agreement patterns.

4. LEXICAL ACCESSIBILITY (LOW-FREQUENCY AVOIDANCE):
   - In spoken Indian languages, speakers prefer high-frequency words. Low-frequency
     words (formal, literary, textbook) are typically replaced with English or
     common everyday equivalents. This is a core motivation for code-mixing.
   - Before using a native word, ask: would a native speaker say this in casual
     conversation, or would they use English or a simpler word? If the word
     sounds like it belongs in a book, news, or formal speech—DISCARD it. Use
     English or a high-frequency native equivalent instead.
   - Domains where English is standard in spoken use: education (exam, result,
     study, mark), emotions (feel, stress, problem, tension), technology (phone,
     bus, computer), career (job, interview). Use English in these domains.
   - Prefer everyday verbs (feel, think, get, say) over long, formal ones.

5. AVOID STRUCTURAL TRANSFER:
   - LLMs often solve tasks in English then translate—this produces
     translationese and ungrammatical output. Generate directly in the target
     language using its grammatical frame.
   - Prefer verbs and everyday phrases over abstract nouns.

6. SPOKEN vs WRITTEN:
   - Use colloquial, spoken style—not formal or textbook. Spoken language
     allows morphophonemic reduction, fragmentary structure, and pro-drop.
   - Use natural code-mixing where appropriate (English loanwords in domains
     like education, technology, emotions where native speakers do).
   - NEVER use verbal fillers like "um", "uh", or "err". Show hesitation or
     difficulty speaking through natural pauses, repetition of native words,
     or fragmented sentences.
